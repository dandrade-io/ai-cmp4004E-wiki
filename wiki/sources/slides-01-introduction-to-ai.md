# Slides 01 — Introduction to AI

**Source:** `raw/slides/01_Introduction to Artificial Intelligence.pptx` (19 slides; credit to Luciana Valdivieso).

## Summary
A timeline tour from ancient automata to LLMs/agents. Starts with "what is intelligence / AI?" (slide 2), then walks through milestones: Talos (~800 BC, slide 19), Antikythera mechanism (200 BC, slides 17–18), Mechanical Turk and Jaquet-Droz automata (1700s, slide 3), Z1 and ENIAC (slide 4), the Turing Test 1950 (slide 5), LISP/FORTRAN (slide 6), Dartmouth 1956 (slide 7), perceptron 1958 (slide 8), Prolog 1970 (slide 9), backprop (slide 11), SVM 1995 (slide 12), deep learning (slide 13), then limits of statistical models and causal models (slides 14–15).

## Key takeaways
- Models are mathematical hypotheses, built by formal methods (first-order calculus, lambda calculus, temporal logic, rewriting, causal calculus) or statistical methods (regression, classification, Bayesian/Markov networks) (slide 10).
- Statistical models have limits; causality is proposed as the next connection to ML ([[causal-models]]).

## Errata / inconsistencies
> ⚠️ Errata: Slide 9 says Prolog "was created by Dennis Ritchie at Bell Labs" and is "an imperative, compiled language". Both are wrong: that describes C. Prolog was created by Colmerauer and Roussel (Marseille, 1972) with Kowalski's theory, and is declarative logic programming (see [[slides-xx-logic-programming-prolog]]).
> ⚠️ Errata: Slide 11 dates backpropagation to 1975 and slide 13 says "deep learning" was introduced in 1986; popularization of backprop is usually dated 1986 (Rumelhart, Hinton, Williams) and the term "deep learning" to the 2000s (unverified).
> Slide 7's "Darthmouth" is a typo for Dartmouth. Slides 14 and 16 contain only images (no extractable text).
